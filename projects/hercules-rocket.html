Hercules Model Rocket

Hercules is a rocket I designed myself in OpenRocket and paid for out of pocket. The design is done and all the parts are bought; the build is next.

What it is and why I did it

I wanted a project that goes through the full engineering loop: model it, simulate it, build it, measure it, and revise. I call it a program rather than a single launch because the goal is a repeatable airframe I can keep improving between builds.

My role

Everything: design, analysis, sourcing parts, and soon the build and flight testing.

Process

  1. Airframe and materials. 41.6 mm (BT-60) paper body tube, plastic nose cone, fins cut from 1/8 in. balsa sheet, an 18 mm heavy-duty motor mount for an Estes C6-5 motor, and a plastic parachute with recovery wadding.
  2. Stability. A rocket only flies straight if its center of pressure (where the aerodynamic forces act) is behind its center of gravity. I used the Barrowman method to calculate where the CP sits, then changed fin size and shape until the CP was 6.2 cm behind the CG: 1.48 body diameters, or “calibers.” 1–2 calibers is the target. Less and it can tumble; much more and it turns into the wind.
  3. Performance prediction. I ran OpenRocket’s flight simulation with the C6-5 motor to predict apogee (174 m), peak speed (64.5 m/s, Mach 0.19), and peak acceleration (148 m/s²).
  4. Recovery. The parachute ejects off a shock cord, timed to the motor’s 5-second delay so it opens near apogee.
  5. Documentation. I wrote up the design rationale and predictions in a technical report.

Results and what’s next

All parts are on hand. Next: assemble the rocket, fly it, and compare the measured apogee against the 174 m prediction. This page will become the build log.
